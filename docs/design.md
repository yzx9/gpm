# gpm Design Principles

> Status: Design direction
> Scope: Brand identity, visual language, UI/UX principles, interaction tone, and design quality
> Excludes: Product requirements, feature specifications, technical architecture, and implementation details

---

## 1. Design Intent

gpm should feel like a private instrument that belongs to its user.

It should not feel like a cloud service, a security dashboard, or a generic “cybersecurity” product. It should feel calm, deliberate, self-contained, and trustworthy—an application that protects sensitive information without dramatizing that protection.

The central design idea is:

> **A modern seal for user-controlled secrets.**

The product should communicate three qualities at the same time:

- **Sealed** — sensitive information is protected and intentionally revealed.
- **Sovereign** — control remains with the user, not with a platform.
- **Verifiable** — trust comes from clear state and understandable behavior, not from decoration.

The visual system draws from Chinese sealing traditions, especially seal impressions and clay seals, but translates those ideas into a modern digital interface. The result should not look antique, ceremonial, or ornamental. It should feel contemporary, useful, and culturally grounded.

---

## 2. Desired User Experience

The interface should make users feel:

### Calm

The product should avoid visual noise, unnecessary urgency, and exaggerated security language. Sensitive actions should feel controlled rather than alarming.

### In control

The interface should make ownership visible. Users should feel that the application is helping them use their own protected information, not granting temporary access to a remote service.

### Respected

The design should not assume that users are careless, uninformed, or incapable. It should explain important states clearly without becoming patronizing.

### Confident

Trust should come from consistency:

- clear hierarchy;
- predictable actions;
- precise wording;
- visible system state;
- reversible interaction where possible;
- honest presentation of uncertainty.

### Protected, but not trapped

Protection should feel strong without suggesting enclosure by the application itself. The interface should communicate that secrets are sealed by the user’s authority, not locked inside a proprietary system.

### Focused

The interface should support one clear task at a time. It should not resemble an administrative console filled with competing panels, metrics, badges, and security scores.

---

## 3. Brand Concept: The Sovereign Seal

The gpm identity is based on a modernized seal impression.

Historically, a seal can represent:

- identity;
- authorship;
- authorization;
- integrity;
- possession;
- confidentiality;
- evidence that something has been opened or altered.

These meanings are more relevant to gpm than common security symbols such as shields, padlocks, fingerprints, or keyholes.

The seal is not merely a metaphor for “locked.” It is a sign that something was intentionally closed by a known holder and can be recognized as belonging to them.

The brand statement is:

> **Cinnabar establishes the seal. Celadon activates the clasp.**

---

## 4. Logo Direction

## 4.1 Primary Form

The primary logo is a square seal impression with softly rounded corners.

Its structure should include:

- a compact square outer boundary;
- an internal path system inspired by seal carving;
- a protected central form;
- a restrained clasp entering from the lower-right edge;
- a small, deliberate circular opening in the seal boundary.

The logo must not be constructed from the letter “G.”

The name gpm originated from “GoPass Mobile,” but the product identity should not imply that it is written in the Go programming language. The logo should therefore be semantic rather than typographic.

It should be recognizable as:

1. a seal;
2. a protected structure;
3. a controlled point of access.

It should not need to be read as an initial.

---

## 4.2 The Seal Clasp

The lower-right secondary form is called the **seal clasp**.

It should consist of:

- a celadon-green circle;
- a short rounded rectangular extension;
- a corresponding circular mouth in the cinnabar seal boundary;
- a narrow and controlled separation between clasp and seal.

The clasp should appear to enter the seal structure, but it must not directly merge with it.

This separation is important. It communicates that access requires alignment and authorization rather than permanent attachment.

The opening in the seal must remain small and intentional. It must never suggest:

- a broken border;
- an unfinished shape;
- casual access;
- an open door;
- a weak enclosure.

The clasp should feel precise, measured, and compatible with the boundary—not improvised.

### Meaning of the clasp

The clasp represents:

- the holder’s authority to open;
- a controlled access point;
- the relationship between identity and sealed content;
- activation without loss of ownership;
- connection without platform dependence.

The clasp is not a literal key, port, plug, or synchronization icon.

---

## 4.3 Shape Principles

The logo should follow these rules:

- The outer silhouette should remain compact and stable.
- Corners should be softened, but not inflated into a generic app-icon shape.
- Internal strokes should share a consistent visual weight.
- Negative space should remain open enough to survive reduction.
- The clasp should remain visually secondary to the full seal.
- The logo should work in one color before color is added.
- Texture may soften the impression but must not define the geometry.
- The mark must remain recognizable without shadows, highlights, gradients, or three-dimensional rendering.

The logo should feel stamped, not illustrated.

---

## 4.4 Seal Texture

The seal should retain a subtle impression-like texture.

Recommended characteristics:

- slightly uneven edges;
- small variations in ink coverage;
- restrained grain;
- occasional tiny gaps in the printed surface;
- a natural but controlled impression.

Avoid:

- heavy cracking;
- distressed poster effects;
- simulated aging;
- dramatic erosion;
- photographic clay texture;
- embossing;
- bevels;
- lighting-based depth;
- obvious paper fibers inside the logo itself.

The geometry must remain clear when texture is removed.

A clean vector master should always exist. Texture should be applied as an optional rendering treatment.

---

## 4.5 Logo Color Hierarchy

The logo is primarily cinnabar.

Recommended visual ratio:

- **Cinnabar:** 88–94%
- **Celadon:** 6–12%

Cinnabar defines the seal as a whole. Celadon identifies the clasp and controlled activation point.

Celadon should not divide the logo into two equal color regions. The mark should still read as a single seal, not as a two-color technology symbol.

---

## 4.6 Logo Variants

The identity system should include:

### Full-color light version

- warm paper background;
- dark cinnabar seal;
- deep celadon clasp.

### Full-color dark version

- ink-black background;
- lifted cinnabar seal;
- lighter celadon clasp.

### Single-color cinnabar version

Used where the cultural identity of the seal is most important.

### Single-color neutral version

Used for system monochrome modes, notification icons, engraving, embossing, or restricted reproduction.

### Reverse version

A light mark on an ink-dark background. The clasp may remain distinguishable by shape rather than color.

### Small-size version

At very small sizes:

- reduce texture;
- simplify internal paths;
- enlarge critical negative spaces slightly;
- preserve the circular mouth;
- retain the clasp silhouette;
- remove nonessential micro-detail.

---

## 5. Color Philosophy

The system uses three layers of color:

1. **Brand color**
2. **Interaction color**
3. **Semantic color**

These roles must remain independent.

---

## 5.1 Cinnabar: Identity and Seal

Cinnabar is the primary brand color.

It represents:

- the seal;
- ownership;
- authority;
- commitment;
- intentional closure;
- cultural identity.

Cinnabar should be used with restraint. It gains meaning through scarcity.

Recommended uses:

- the primary logo;
- brand moments;
- seal-like status marks;
- completion of identity-establishing flows;
- selected illustrations;
- limited accents that communicate ownership or sealing.

Cinnabar should not automatically be used for:

- every primary button;
- every active control;
- destructive actions;
- validation errors;
- warning banners;
- general navigation.

Brand cinnabar and semantic danger red must use separate tokens and visibly distinct values.

### Suggested light-theme cinnabar

```css
--seal-600: #8f3f36;
--seal-700: #74312b;
--seal-500: #a95145;
--seal-container: #f3e4e0;
--on-seal-container: #5b241f;
```

### Suggested dark-theme cinnabar

```css
--seal-dark-600: #cf796d;
--seal-dark-700: #b96559;
--seal-dark-500: #e18f82;
--seal-dark-container: #4c2925;
--on-seal-dark-container: #ffdad4;
```

The dark-theme cinnabar is intentionally lighter. The color role remains the same, but its luminance changes so it does not disappear into the background.

---

## 5.2 Celadon: Order and Activation

Celadon is the primary interaction color.

Its cultural reference comes from the restrained, quiet quality of Chinese celadon rather than from bright modern green. It should feel mineral, muted, stable, and enduring.

It represents:

- controlled activation;
- usable state;
- focus;
- navigation;
- continuity;
- connection;
- calm operational confidence.

Recommended uses:

- primary actions;
- selected controls;
- focus rings;
- active navigation;
- toggles;
- links;
- the seal clasp;
- controlled progress;
- normal connected states;
- subtle path or node illustrations.

Celadon is not a universal “success green.” Long-term stable states may use it, but success, warning, danger, and information should remain separate semantic categories.

### Suggested light-theme celadon

```css
--celadon-600: #4d6b61;
--celadon-700: #3f5f57;
--celadon-500: #5f7d73;
--celadon-container: #dce8e3;
--on-celadon-container: #203a32;
```

### Suggested dark-theme celadon

```css
--celadon-dark-600: #88ada1;
--celadon-dark-700: #71968b;
--celadon-dark-500: #9ac1b6;
--celadon-dark-container: #253d36;
--on-celadon-dark-container: #c4e1d8;
```

---

## 5.3 Neutrals: The Space That Holds Secrets

Most of the interface should be neutral.

Neutrals provide:

- visual quiet;
- legibility;
- stable hierarchy;
- space for sensitive content;
- contrast for small amounts of brand color.

The interface should not be filled with cinnabar and celadon. Their impact depends on a calm neutral field.

### Light theme

The light theme should use a warm paper-like background.

It should suggest paper through color temperature, not through obvious texture.

```css
--canvas: #f5f3ee;
--surface-primary: #fcfbf8;
--surface-secondary: #ecefea;
--surface-raised: #ffffff;

--text-primary: #1b201d;
--text-secondary: #5b645f;
--text-tertiary: #7b837e;

--border-default: #d8ddd8;
--border-strong: #bec6c1;
```

Avoid:

- yellow antique paper;
- visible fibers behind text;
- stains;
- aged edges;
- decorative parchment effects.

The surface should feel warm, not historical.

### Dark theme

The dark theme should use an ink-like dark neutral rather than pure black.

```css
--canvas: #111613;
--surface-primary: #181e1a;
--surface-secondary: #202823;
--surface-raised: #28312b;

--text-primary: #eef2ed;
--text-secondary: #afbab3;
--text-tertiary: #849088;

--border-default: #344039;
--border-strong: #46544c;
```

The dark theme should feel like ink, stone, or lacquered darkness—not a neon terminal.

Pure black may be offered as an optional OLED treatment, but it should not define the core brand presentation.

---

## 5.4 Color Balance

A typical product screen should approximately follow this balance:

- **Neutral surfaces and text:** 75–85%
- **Celadon interaction color:** 10–18%
- **Cinnabar brand color:** 3–8%

A screen usually should not allow both brand colors to dominate simultaneously.

Examples:

- Daily working screens should be led by neutrals and celadon.
- Brand introduction or completion moments may give cinnabar greater presence.
- The logo may use both, but cinnabar remains dominant.
- Warning and danger states should override the brand palette with semantic colors where clarity requires it.

---

## 5.5 Light and Dark Theme Relationship

The dark theme is not an inversion of the light theme.

Both themes preserve the same role system:

|Role|Light Theme|Dark Theme|
|-|-|-|
|Seal / identity|Dark cinnabar|Lifted cinnabar|
|Action / activation|Deep celadon|Light celadon|
|Canvas|Warm paper|Ink-black|
|Main text|Near-black|Soft white|
|Surfaces|Warm white|Layered charcoal-green|

The hue relationship remains recognizable, but luminance is adjusted for contrast and comfort.

The dark theme should not make cinnabar look brown or celadon look muddy. Both colors need dedicated dark-theme values.

---

## 6. UI Character

The UI should be:

- restrained;
- deliberate;
- tactile without being skeuomorphic;
- modern without being generic;
- culturally informed without becoming decorative;
- dense enough to feel like a tool;
- spacious enough to protect attention.

It should avoid the visual language of:

- financial dashboards;
- antivirus software;
- enterprise administration;
- game interfaces;
- cyberpunk terminals;
- luxury cultural merchandise;
- government seals;
- antique document reproduction.

---

## 7. Core UI/UX Principles

## 7.1 Security Should Be Experienced as Clarity

Users should feel protected because the application behaves consistently and communicates state precisely.

Security should be expressed through:

- deliberate reveal;
- predictable concealment;
- clear confirmation;
- visible boundaries;
- precise language;
- understandable state changes;
- restrained use of sensitive information.

Do not rely on:

- repeated padlock icons;
- shields on every screen;
- aggressive warnings;
- “military-grade” language;
- dark mode as a security signal;
- decorative encryption graphics.

---

## 7.2 Plaintext Is a Temporary State

Sensitive information should visually feel sealed by default.

When revealed, the interface should communicate that the state is temporary and intentional.

The interaction tone should be:

- quiet;
- focused;
- reversible;
- time-aware;
- free from spectacle.

Avoid dramatic opening animations, glowing secrets, or effects that attract unnecessary attention.

A good reveal interaction should feel like a controlled unsealing, not like opening a treasure chest.

---

## 7.3 One Primary Action Per Context

Each view should make its main action obvious.

Secondary actions should remain available without competing for attention.

Avoid:

- multiple equally weighted full-width buttons;
- destructive actions beside routine actions;
- large collections of colored action cards;
- menus that present every capability at once.

Hierarchy should be visible before reading.

---

## 7.4 Progressive Disclosure

Simple language should come first.

Technical detail should be available, but not forced into the primary path.

The interface should reveal complexity in layers:

1. human meaning;
2. current state;
3. available action;
4. technical details.

This allows the design to remain approachable without hiding important facts.

---

## 7.5 State Must Be Honest

The interface should distinguish between different kinds of certainty.

It should never collapse several conditions into a vague “secure” or “unsafe” label.

State design should answer:

- What is happening?
- What does it affect?
- Does the user need to act?
- Is the condition temporary?
- Can the state be verified?
- What is the next safe action?

Use text, iconography, and hierarchy together. Do not rely on color alone.

---

## 7.6 Local Ownership Should Be Visible

The interface should consistently suggest that the user is working with a private collection that exists under their control.

The product should avoid language and visual patterns that imply:

- account tenancy;
- subscription ownership;
- platform custody;
- a remote service as the source of truth;
- access being granted by gpm itself.

The application is a tool used by the owner, not the owner of the data.

---

## 7.7 Safe States Should Feel Quiet

A normal protected state should not demand attention.

Successful protection is the background condition of the product.

Reserve strong emphasis for:

- a required decision;
- a meaningful transition;
- a genuine warning;
- an irreversible action;
- a brand-defining moment.

“Everything is normal” should feel stable, not celebratory.

---

## 7.8 Problems Should Feel Recoverable

Errors should not make the application feel broken or unsafe by default.

The design should:

- preserve user context;
- explain impact;
- separate temporary failure from data loss;
- provide a clear next action;
- avoid blame;
- avoid exposing unnecessary technical detail.

Warning language should be factual rather than dramatic.

---

## 7.9 The Product Should Feel Precise, Not Mechanical

Precision comes from alignment, spacing, wording, and predictable behavior.

It does not require:

- grid-heavy technical decoration;
- monospaced type everywhere;
- visible protocol symbols;
- terminal-like interfaces;
- excessively sharp geometry.

The seal identity introduces cultural warmth. The interface should balance that warmth with modern precision.

---

## 8. Typography

Use the platform system sans-serif for interface text.

The default type system should feel:

- neutral;
- highly readable;
- contemporary;
- stable across languages;
- suitable for long-term use.

Use monospaced type only where character distinction is materially useful.

Recommended use of monospaced type:

- secret values when revealed;
- fingerprints;
- hashes;
- technical identifiers;
- file-like paths;
- code-like strings.

Do not use monospaced type as the main brand voice.

### Typographic hierarchy

Use a small, disciplined set of roles:

- Display
- Page title
- Section title
- Body
- Supporting text
- Label
- Monospaced technical text

Avoid:

- frequent all-caps;
- decorative pseudo-calligraphy;
- imitation seal script in body text;
- overly thin text;
- low-contrast secondary labels;
- excessive size variation.

Chinese cultural identity should come from structure, color, and material reference—not from making the entire interface look historical.

---

## 9. Layout and Spatial Rhythm

Use a 4 dp base spacing system.

Recommended rhythm:

- 4 dp — micro spacing;
- 8 dp — icon-to-label;
- 12 dp — compact internal spacing;
- 16 dp — standard page and component spacing;
- 24 dp — section separation;
- 32 dp — major structural separation.

The interface should feel measured rather than airy for its own sake.

### Touch targets

Interactive targets should be at least 48 × 48 dp.

Visual icons may be smaller, but touch areas should remain generous.

### Content width

Long-form content should avoid stretching across very wide screens.

Use constrained reading widths and multi-pane layouts where appropriate.

---

## 10. Shape Language

The shape system should echo the seal without turning every element into a stamp.

### Recommended corner behavior

- Buttons and fields: 10–12 dp
- Cards and grouped containers: 12–16 dp
- Bottom sheets: 24 dp at top corners
- Status capsules: fully rounded
- List rows: flat or subtly rounded
- App icon: platform-adaptive

Avoid excessive pill shapes and oversized rounded cards.

The seal logo may carry soft irregularity. UI components should remain geometrically controlled.

---

## 11. Texture and Material

Texture belongs primarily to brand expression, not to functional interface surfaces.

### Appropriate uses

- app icon;
- launch screen;
- brand illustration;
- empty-state artwork;
- documentation cover;
- marketing assets.

### Inappropriate uses

- text backgrounds;
- input fields;
- settings lists;
- data-heavy pages;
- all cards;
- interactive states;
- every surface in the application.

The product should suggest material culture without simulating physical objects.

---

## 12. Iconography

Icons should use:

- consistent stroke weight;
- rounded terminals;
- simple silhouettes;
- minimal interior detail;
- clear alignment;
- 20–24 dp standard sizes.

Security symbols should be used only when semantically accurate.

A lock should mean locked.  
A shield should mean verified integrity or protection.  
A key should mean identity or access.  
A connection symbol should mean an actual relationship or path.

Do not use security icons as decorative reassurance.

The seal clasp should remain a brand element and should not become a universal icon for every action.

---

## 13. Motion

Motion should reinforce controlled state change.

Recommended qualities:

- short;
- quiet;
- directional;
- physically plausible;
- easy to interrupt;
- respectful of reduced-motion settings.

### Brand motion

A suitable brand animation may show:

1. the cinnabar seal appearing as a single impression;
2. the celadon clasp aligning with the circular mouth;
3. a brief controlled activation;
4. the interface becoming available.

The clasp should not fly dramatically into place or produce light effects.

### Sensitive-state motion

Reveal and conceal transitions should be subtle and immediate.

Concealment should take priority over animation completion.

### Timing guidance

- Micro-interactions: 120–180 ms
- Navigation: 200–240 ms
- Sheets and dialogs: 220–280 ms
- Conceal or lock: immediate or under 100 ms

Avoid:

- bouncing;
- pulsing sensitive values;
- celebratory confetti;
- glowing paths;
- ornamental ink-spread effects during routine use.

---

## 14. Language and Tone

The product voice should be:

- direct;
- calm;
- factual;
- respectful;
- precise;
- non-promotional.

It should explain consequences, not merely label states.

Prefer:

> Your content remains sealed.

Over:

> Maximum security enabled.

Prefer:

> This state has not been verified.

Over:

> Security risk detected!

Prefer:

> Reveal for 10 seconds.

Over:

> Unlock secret.

Avoid absolute claims such as:

- unbreakable;
- impossible to hack;
- military-grade;
- completely safe;
- zero risk;
- permanently secure.

The brand metaphor may use words such as “sealed,” “unsealed,” or “mark,” but the interface should not become excessively poetic or archaic.

---

## 15. Accessibility

Accessibility is part of the design language, not an additional mode.

Requirements:

- text and interactive controls must meet contrast targets;
- state must never rely on color alone;
- controls must have meaningful accessible labels;
- large text must preserve hierarchy and action clarity;
- focus states must remain visible in both themes;
- reduced-motion preferences must be respected;
- technical strings must wrap, scroll, or copy safely;
- icon-only actions must remain understandable;
- sensitive values must not be exposed unintentionally to assistive output.

The seal texture must never reduce logo recognition or text legibility.

---

## 16. Dark Theme Principles

The dark theme should preserve warmth and cultural identity.

It should not become:

- pure black with neon accents;
- a terminal aesthetic;
- a high-contrast gaming theme;
- a red warning environment.

Use ink-like layered surfaces.

Cinnabar and celadon must be lifted in luminance, not simply reused from the light theme.

The dark theme should remain visually quiet even when both brand colors are present.

Recommended behavior:

- cinnabar remains the seal;
- celadon remains activation;
- neutral ink surfaces dominate;
- white is softened slightly;
- borders are visible but restrained;
- large areas of bright accent color are avoided.

---

## 17. Light Theme Principles

The light theme should feel clear, warm, and grounded.

The paper reference should come from color temperature rather than imitation texture.

It should not feel:

- yellowed;
- nostalgic;
- decorative;
- rustic;
- like stationery.

Use warm off-white as the canvas and near-white for raised surfaces.

Cinnabar should appear natural against the background. Celadon should remain muted and functional.

---

## 18. Design Anti-Patterns

Do not use:

- a letter “G” as the central logo idea;
- shields or padlocks as the brand mark;
- bright red and green in equal visual weight;
- large gaps in the seal border;
- a clasp that looks like a USB plug, connector, or external badge;
- gradients as the primary identity;
- shadows to explain the seal structure;
- photorealistic clay;
- fake historical aging;
- cyberpunk styling;
- neon green;
- dashboard-like security scores;
- excessive cards;
- colored borders around every component;
- decorative Chinese motifs without semantic purpose;
- seals, stamps, and calligraphy on every screen;
- semantic danger red as brand cinnabar;
- celadon as a universal success color;
- dark mode as proof of security.

---

## 19. Design Review Questions

Every major design should be reviewed using the following questions:

1. Does the screen feel calm and controlled?
2. Is ownership visible without being repeatedly explained?
3. Is the primary action obvious?
4. Are brand color and semantic color being confused?
5. Is cinnabar being used sparingly enough to retain meaning?
6. Is celadon supporting action rather than decoration?
7. Does the dark theme preserve contrast without becoming neon?
8. Does the light theme feel warm without looking antique?
9. Is sensitive information visually sealed by default?
10. Does a reveal state feel intentional and temporary?
11. Are warnings factual and recoverable?
12. Is technical complexity progressively disclosed?
13. Does the design rely on a lock or shield where clearer language would be better?
14. Is texture supporting the brand without reducing legibility?
15. Does the clasp feel integrated, precise, and controlled?
16. Is the seal opening small enough to avoid suggesting weak boundaries?
17. Does the interface remain understandable without color?
18. Does the design still work at large text sizes?
19. Does the visual hierarchy survive in monochrome?
20. Does the result feel like a modern instrument rather than a themed cultural artifact?

---

## 20. North Star

The ideal gpm experience should feel like handling a private seal:

- deliberate when opened;
- quiet when closed;
- unmistakably owned;
- stable over time;
- protected without spectacle;
- culturally grounded without nostalgia;
- modern without becoming generic.

The final design should leave users with one simple impression:

> **My secrets remain under my authority. gpm helps me use them without taking ownership away from me.**
